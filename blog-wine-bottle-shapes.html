Choosing the Right
Wine Bottle Shape

Spirits & Wine · 6 min read

The bottle shape you choose says almost as much about your wine as the label does. But beyond first impressions, shape affects cost, shelf behaviour, and how your product performs from bottling line to dinner table. Here's what actually matters when you're deciding.

Bordeaux: the safe, versatile default

Straight sides and high shoulders make the Bordeaux shape the most widely used wine bottle in the world — and for good reason. The high shoulder helps catch sediment in reds that throw deposits with age, and the straight-sided design stacks and labels easily on standard production lines. If you're not sure which shape fits your wine, Bordeaux is rarely the wrong answer.

Burgundy: softer lines, different signal

The Burgundy bottle's sloped shoulders and wider base give it a different visual weight on the shelf — often associated with Pinot Noir, Chardonnay, and Rhône varietals. It doesn't offer the sediment-catching shoulder of a Bordeaux, so it's less common for wines intended for long cellaring, but it reads as elegant and slightly less formal, which some brands use deliberately.

Custom shapes: when it's worth it

A custom silhouette signals premium positioning immediately, before anyone reads the label. It's the right call when:

  • Shelf differentiation matters more than cost. If you're competing in a crowded price tier, a distinctive shape earns a second look.
  • Your volumes support tooling costs. Custom moulds carry upfront investment that only makes sense at meaningful order volumes.
  • You have lead time to spare. Custom development — from sketch to mould to first production run — takes materially longer than ordering from stock.

If those conditions aren't met yet, a stock shape in a distinctive colour or with custom decoration (embossing, printed labels, unique capsules) often achieves 80% of the shelf impact for a fraction of the cost and lead time.

Weight and cost considerations

Heavier glass has long been used as a shorthand for premium quality, but it also raises freight costs meaningfully — especially relevant if you're exporting. Lightweighted bottles have improved enough in recent years that many premium brands now use them without any perceived drop in quality, so it's worth asking your supplier what weight options exist within a given shape before assuming heavier is necessary.

Closure compatibility

Bottle shape and finish (the top of the neck) need to match your intended closure — cork, screw cap, or synthetic stopper all require specific neck finishes and tolerances. This is worth confirming early, particularly for custom shapes, since a mismatch between bottle finish and closure discovered after tooling is cut is an expensive mistake to fix.
